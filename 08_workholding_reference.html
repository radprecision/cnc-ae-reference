🔧 Workholding Reference

CNC AE Reference Library — Module 22 · 3-2-1 locating · Types · Clamping forces · Failure diagnostics

3
Primary datum
3 points on primary plane → constrains Z translation + rotation about X and Y (3 DOF)
2
Secondary datum
2 points on secondary plane → constrains Y translation + rotation about Z (2 DOF)
1
Tertiary datum
1 point on tertiary plane → constrains X translation (1 DOF)
Four support points = rocking chairThree points define a plane — always. Four points overdetermine it and the part rocks between two stable positions. Always use three datum pads in a large triangle for the primary face.
ViolationWhat happens
Under-constrained (<6 DOF)Part moves during machining. Features out of position. Inconsistent part-to-part.
Over-constrained (redundant locators)Part rocks. Inconsistent seating. Part distorts to contact all locators — stress releases when unclamped.
Clamping toward locators ✓Correct — clamp force pushes part into locating surfaces. Always do this.
Clamping away from locators ✗Part lifts off primary datum under cutting forces. Chatter and dimensional error.

Vise — Kurt / precision milling vise

  • Best for: Prismatic parts with parallel faces
  • Jaw lift: Part lifts slightly under jaw load — seat with mallet after clamping
  • Soft jaws: Machine to part profile in the vise — ±0.0005″ repeatability
  • Repeatability: Hard jaws ±0.001–0.003″ · Soft jaws ±0.0005″

Collet chuck

  • Best for: Round/hex bar stock, 360° grip, bar-feed CNC turning
  • 5C collets: Up to 1-1/16″ — must match bar diameter closely
  • Dead-length: Part doesn't move axially when closing — critical for Z reference
  • TIR: 0.0002–0.0005″ typical

3-jaw chuck

  • Self-centering: All jaws move simultaneously — fast setup
  • TIR: 0.002–0.005″ standard jaws · 0.0005–0.001″ precision soft jaws
  • Jaw lift: High RPM opens jaws — observe max RPM ratings

Custom fixture plate

  • Best for: Medium-high volume, complex/irregular parts
  • Locating: 2 dowel pins (1 round, 1 diamond), hardened rest pads
  • Material: Cast iron preferred — stable and damping
  • Repeatability: ±0.0005″ with precision ground components
Soft jaws are underused in most job shopsSoft jaws cost 20–40 minutes to make, dramatically improve repeatability, protect finished surfaces, and allow complex shapes to be held in a standard vise or chuck. Make them the default for any second-operation setup on a finished surface.

Required clamping force

F_clamp ≥ (F_cut × SF) / µ
SF = 2.0 (continuous) or 3.0 (interrupted)
µ steel dry ≈ 0.15 · µ aluminum ≈ 0.40 · µ rubber pad ≈ 0.60

Clamp placement rules

  • Always push part INTO locators — never away
  • Position clamps opposite support points — never over unsupported spans
  • Never clamp on a tolerance-critical finished surface without soft pads
  • Multiple light clamps better than one heavy clamp for thin walls
  • Last clamp must not shift the part — verify with indicator

Over-clamping is as dangerous as under-clampingExcessive clamp force distorts thin-wall parts during machining. The part springs back after unclamping — and the feature dimensions change. A bore that measures correct in the fixture reads oval on the CMM.

Locating

  • 3-2-1 only — never exceed 6 locating points without reason
  • Maximize primary datum triangle footprint
  • All locating surfaces hardened and ground
  • Two dowel pins: one round + one diamond
  • Datum from machined features — never raw stock

Clamping

  • Every clamp pushes part into at least one locator
  • Tighten center-outward, alternating sides
  • Strap clamp bolt closer to part than support stud
  • Specify torque value — not "tighten firmly"
  • Never clamp over an unsupported span

Access & clearance

  • Map every tool path before finalizing fixture design
  • Long-reach tools need shank clearance, not just tip
  • Design chip evacuation paths into fixture
  • Coolant/TSC must reach the cutting zone
  • Operator loading must be safe and clear

Repeatability

  • Air blast before every load cycle — chips cause 80% of seating errors
  • Specify clamp torque in the process sheet
  • Qualify fixture with indicator before first part
  • Inspect datum pads for wear at PM intervals

Thermal effects

  • Match fixture and part material where possible
  • Allow 30–60 min machine warm-up before precision work
  • Use in-process probing to track and correct thermal drift

Documentation

  • Every custom fixture needs a drawing
  • Specify clamp torque in ft-lbs or N·m
  • Specify air blast in NC program — not verbal instruction
  • Fixture ID must match process sheet
MaterialKey challengeClamp strategySpecific rules
Aluminum (6061, 7075)Soft surface — jaw marks. Low modulus — deflects. High thermal expansion.Soft jaws for finished surfaces. Distribute clamp force.Never steel serrated jaws on finish-machined aluminum. Min wall 0.060″ for clamping. Check dims after unclamping.
Alloy steel (4140, 4340)High cutting forces. Hardened parts need non-marring clamps.Standard hard jaws on rough surfaces. Copper/brass pads on finished surfaces.Verify clamp force exceeds 3× cutting force for interrupted cuts. Q&T over 40 HRC — use precision soft jaws.
Stainless (304, 316)Work hardening under clamping. Galling on metal-to-metal contact.Avoid steel-on-stainless contact — use brass/bronze pads. Distribute force.Apply anti-galling compound to locating pin contacts. Do not over-clamp thin-wall stainless.
Titanium (Ti-6Al-4V)Low modulus — significant deflection. Titanium galls on steel.Non-ferrous pads at all contact points. Distributed clamping.Never bare steel clamps on titanium. Verify contact with Mylar shim before first article. Springback 0.002–0.005″ on thin walls.
Inconel / superalloysExtreme cutting forces. Work hardening. High-value parts.Maximum clamp force. Hydraulic clamping preferred. Maximum rigidity.Workholding rigidity is the #1 factor in Inconel machining. Every source of compliance = chatter and poor tool life.
Plastics / compositesLow crush strength. Creep under sustained load. Static charge.Vacuum preferred for flat parts. Large-area soft jaws for irregular shapes. Limit torque to 20–40% of metal equivalent.Measure parts after unclamping — plastics relax and spring back. Composites: never clamp through fibers without backup plate.
Feature position shifts part-to-part
Inconsistent seating between load cycles
Check 1: Chips or coolant under datum pads — this is the most common cause. Add air blast before loading.
Check 2: Worn or loose locating pins — measure pin/pocket clearance, replace if >0.001″.
Check 3: Clamp sequence shifting the part — indicate before/after each clamp is tightened.
Bore or hole is oval / out of round
Part distorted by clamping forces, springs back after unclamping
Check 1: Measure bore in-fixture vs out. If different — clamping distortion confirmed.
Check 2: Reduce clamp force. Distribute clamps around circumference.
Check 3: Add support inside bore (expandable mandrel) or outside (split-ring collet).
Chatter — vibration during cutting
Workholding compliance — part or fixture moving at cut frequency
Check 1: Indicate part during cutting — movement confirms workholding source.
Check 2: Tighten all clamps — insufficient force is most common cause.
Check 3: Check for worn/loose locating components — 0.001″ gap can trigger chatter.
First article passes, production parts fail
Process change between first article and production
Check 1: Same fixture? Same clamp torque? Same operator procedure? Small differences compound.
Check 2: Run a setup verification sheet on the first production setup — not just first article.
Check 3: Inspect fixture for wear between first article and production run.
Workholding problems masquerade as tooling or programming problemsConsistent position error looks like a work offset issue. Dimensional drift looks like tool wear. An oval bore looks like a boring parameter problem. Verify workholding FIRST — indicate the part, check for chips under datum pads, verify clamp torque. It's checked last but should be checked first.
